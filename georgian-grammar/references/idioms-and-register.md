# Idioms, Figurative Language, and Register in Georgian

Many common Georgian phrases carry meaning that a word-by-word reading
won't reach — and some phrases can go either way depending on context.
Handling these well means keeping several layers separate rather than
collapsing straight to one translation.

## The framework

For any phrase that might be more than literal, it helps to track:

- **Literal translation** — a word-for-word rendering, kept only to show
  the phrase's surface form. Never treat this as the final answer for an
  idiomatic phrase.
- **Best/natural translation** — the rendering that actually carries the
  phrase's real meaning, tone, and communicative purpose in the target
  language. There isn't always one single correct choice — alternatives
  are fine when they preserve the same sense.
- **Core meaning** — a plain-language statement of what the phrase
  actually communicates, independent of translation.
- **Interpretation type** — literal, idiomatic, metaphorical,
  value-based/moral, emotional, social, or some mix. Decide this from the
  context the phrase appears in, not from the phrase alone.
- **Emotional / social / moral effect** — what the phrase does
  interpersonally: does it damage trust, offer encouragement, mark a
  boundary, express support, signal conflict, restore a relationship?
  This layer is easy to lose even when the "meaning" is technically
  translated correctly.

## Examples

The idiomatic senses below match the Explanatory Dictionary of the
Georgian Language (ქართული ენის განმარტებითი ლექსიკონი).

- `კარი დახურა` — "closed the door" — plain **literal** use in most
  contexts (e.g. someone actually closing a door)
- `ყველა კარი დახურა` — "closed every door" — **metaphorical**: closed off
  all options or opportunities, not literal doors
- `სიტყვა გატეხა` — literally "broke the word" (`სიტყვა` also means
  "promise") — **value-based**: the real meaning is that someone broke a
  promise and damaged trust. Reading this literally ("physically broke a
  word") or flattening it to something neutral like "changed a decision"
  both lose what the phrase is actually doing — naming a violation of
  trust, with real social and moral weight attached
- `გული არ გაიტეხო` — literally "don't break your heart" — **emotional
  support/encouragement**: don't lose heart, don't lose hope. Flattening
  this to a bare "don't stop" loses the encouraging, sympathetic tone —
  the phrase is doing emotional work, not just describing an action
- `სული მოითქვა` — literally "caught/recovered breath" — genuinely
  **context-dependent**: can be literal (physically caught their breath)
  or figurative (got a respite, recovered after a stressful period);
  decide from context, don't default to one reading
- `ზურგი აქცია` — literally "turned [their] back" — can be purely literal
  (`მონადირემ კლდეს ზურგი აქცია` "the hunter turned his back to the
  cliff" — Vazha-Pshavela, as quoted in the Explanatory Dictionary), but
  said of a person it usually carries a social effect of
  withdrawing support and increasing distance, similar to the English
  idiom "turned their back on"
- `გული სტკივა` — literally "heart hurts" — this is the sharpest example
  of context-dependence: in casual conversation this is almost always
  emotional pain (being grieved or deeply upset about something), but in
  a medical context (a doctor scheduling an exam, a discussion of
  symptoms) it may well be **literal physical pain**. Automatically
  reading it as figurative in a clearly clinical context is itself a
  documented error — figurative language doesn't override context that
  points to a literal reading

## Practical guidance

- Keep literal and best translation as two separate things you could show
  side by side, not one merged guess.
- Don't assume every emotionally- or body-part-referencing phrase is
  idiomatic — check what the surrounding context supports.
- When a phrase's emotional, social, or moral charge is part of its
  point (encouragement, an accusation of betrayal, a boundary being
  drawn), make sure that charge survives translation — a technically
  accurate but flat rendering can still lose the actual message.
- If a phrase's interpretation genuinely depends on context you don't
  have, say so rather than defaulting confidently to one reading.
